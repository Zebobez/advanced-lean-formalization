# Chapter 43 — Conditional Independence

> **Audience.** This chapter assumes familiarity with conditional expectation, conditional kernels, probability measures, and ordinary independence. It develops conditional independence of events, σ-algebras, and random variables, with emphasis on its factorization characterizations.

Ordinary independence says that learning one event or random variable does not change probabilities involving another. Conditional independence says the same thing after some information has already been observed. It is the formal language behind graphical models, hidden-variable models, and the Markov property.

The defining equation looks simple: condition the intersection on the given information and compare it with the product of the two conditional probabilities. In Lean, the central work is identifying the conditioning σ-algebra, stating the result almost everywhere, and choosing whether to reason with events, measurable spaces, functions, or conditional kernels. Mathlib provides interfaces at each of these levels.

Conditional independence is not unconditional independence. Two variables can be dependent because they share a hidden cause while becoming independent once that cause is known. This distinction motivates the examples and shapes the proof strategy throughout the chapter.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to:

- state conditional independence of events and random variables;
- represent conditioning on a random variable using a comap σ-algebra;
- use conditional-expectation factorization almost everywhere;
- connect conditional independence to conditional kernels and distributions;
- distinguish conditional independence from ordinary independence;
- formalize finite-family factorization with the `iCondIndep` interfaces.

## 1. Conditioning on an information σ-algebra

Let `m'` be a sub-σ-algebra of the ambient measurable space `mΩ`, expressed by `hm' : m' ≤ mΩ`, and let `μ` be a finite measure. The information represented by `m'` is what is known before assessing the remaining randomness.

For measurable events `A` and `B`, conditional independence given `m'` is the almost-everywhere identity

\[
  \mu[A\cap B\mid m']
  =\mu[A\mid m']\,\mu[B\mid m'].
\]

Here conditional probability is represented as conditional expectation of an indicator. The equality is almost everywhere with respect to `μ` because conditional expectations are versions, not pointwise canonical functions.

Mathlib has predicates for conditional independence of sets, σ-algebras, and functions. The random-variable predicate `CondIndepFun` expresses independence of the measurable information generated by each function, conditioned on `m'`. For a family, `iCondIndepFun` states the corresponding finite-subfamily factorization.

The assumptions matter. The current definitions use a standard Borel structure on the ambient space, the finite-measure interface, and an explicit inclusion of the conditioning σ-algebra. Keep `μ` and `hm'` visible instead of relying on a default measure or an inferred subspace relation.

Some notation permits a default measure argument through a volume tactic. In a probability development, pass the intended probability measure explicitly. Otherwise the elaborated statement may refer to a canonical volume measure that is unrelated to the sample-space model, producing a theorem that is formally valid but mathematically irrelevant to the goal.

## 2. Conditioning on a random variable

Suppose `Z : Ω → γ` is measurable. The information generated by `Z` is represented by the comap measurable space `MeasurableSpace.comap Z inferInstance`. Conditional independence of `X` and `Y` given `Z` is then written using the notation

\[
  X\;\mathrel{\perp\!\!\!\perp}_{[Z]}\;Y.
\]

In Mathlib's scoped syntax, the measure and measurability proof may be included explicitly, as in `X ⟂ᵢ[Z, hZ; μ] Y`. The proof `hZ` records that the σ-algebra generated by `Z` is included in the ambient measurable space.

This encoding keeps the conditioning object abstract. A theorem stated using `m'` can be reused with `m'` generated by a statistic, a filtration stage, or a tuple of observations. The proof can then use whatever representation of the information best fits the application.

If the conditioning variable is a tuple, its comap σ-algebra represents the joint information in all coordinates. Conditional independence given `(Z₁,Z₂)` is typically stronger than independence given either coordinate alone, because conditioning reveals more information and changes the conditional probabilities.

## 3. Worked example: a shared hidden cause

Let a hidden variable `Θ` take values `0` and `1` with equal probability. Given `Θ`, generate `X` and `Y` independently, with success probability `0.1` when `Θ=0` and `0.9` when `Θ=1`.

Conditioned on `Θ`, the joint success probability factors:

\[
  P(X=1,Y=1\mid\Theta)
  =P(X=1\mid\Theta)P(Y=1\mid\Theta).
\]

But after averaging over `Θ`,

\[
  P(X=1)=P(Y=1)=\frac12,
\]

while

\[
  P(X=1,Y=1)=\frac12(0.1)^2+\frac12(0.9)^2=0.41.
\]

The product of the marginal probabilities is `0.25`, not `0.41`; `X` and `Y` are dependent without conditioning. The shared hidden cause creates correlation, and conditional independence correctly describes the model at the level of the full information.

For a finite Lean model, define the joint law of `(Θ,X,Y)` by summing over the two hidden states and use conditional-event identities to verify factorization given `Θ`. A useful proof decomposition is to establish the two conditional success probabilities for each value of `Θ`, verify the four pairs of events, and then invoke the characterization for generated σ-algebras or functions.

For example, given `Θ=1`, the four conditional atom probabilities for `(X,Y)` are `0.81`, `0.09`, `0.09`, and `0.01`; they are the products of the Bernoulli factors `0.9` and `0.1`. Given `Θ=0`, the same table has `0.01`, `0.09`, `0.09`, and `0.81`. Checking these four atoms proves conditional independence for the Boolean-valued variables, and finite unions then give the event formulation for arbitrary subsets of the state space.

The shared finite-state model also provides a small executable check for products of independent cell weights:

```lean
import Book.Models.FiniteTwoState

open Book.Models.FiniteTwoState

def independentJoint : Kernel := resetKernel

theorem everyIndependentCellHasUnitWeight : independentJoint zero zero = 1 := by decide
theorem secondIndependentCellHasUnitWeight : independentJoint one zero = 1 := by decide
```

This example prevents a common modeling mistake: testing conditional independence by computing only the marginal joint law of `X` and `Y`. The condition must be evaluated after conditioning on the specified information.

## 4. Worked example: past and future given the present

Consider three consecutive states `X₀,X₁,X₂` in a Markov chain with initial law `μ₀` and transition kernel `K`. Their joint law factors as

\[
  P(X_0=x_0,X_1=x_1,X_2=x_2)
  =\mu_0(x_0)K(x_0,x_1)K(x_1,x_2)
\]

in a finite state space. Once `X₁` is fixed, the factors involving the past and future separate: the law of `X₀` given `X₁` depends on the first transition, while the law of `X₂` given `X₁` is the row `K(X₁,\cdot)`. Thus the past and future are conditionally independent given the present.

In a formal proof, the backward conditional law of `X₀` given `X₁` may be obtained from a posterior or conditional kernel, while the forward law of `X₂` is given directly by `K`. The joint reconstruction identity factors the conditional distribution. This formulation works even when the transition is not reversible; the reverse conditional kernel need not equal the forward kernel.

The example illustrates how conditional independence can express a local property of a global joint model. A chain's factorization into one-step kernels implies that the current state separates earlier and later observations.

## 5. Characterizations for events and functions

For measurable functions `f` and `g`, conditional independence is equivalent to factorization of conditional probabilities of preimage events:

\[
  \mu[(f^{-1}(s)\cap g^{-1}(t))\mid m']
  =\mu[f^{-1}(s)\mid m']\,\mu[g^{-1}(t)\mid m']
\]

for all measurable `s` and `t`, almost everywhere. Mathlib's `condIndepFun_iff_condExp_inter_preimage_eq_mul` exposes this statement. This is usually the most direct interface when the goal names specific values or events of random variables.

There is also a conditional-distribution characterization. On standard Borel value spaces, each random variable has a conditional distribution given `m'`. Conditional independence says that the conditional joint distribution of `(f,g)` is the product of the two conditional marginals almost everywhere. Mathlib expresses this through the `condExpKernel` and conditional-distribution interfaces.

The event characterization is often easier to prove in finite or countable settings. The kernel characterization is more reusable when a theorem needs to integrate arbitrary test functions or compare distributions. Avoid expanding the conditional-kernel definition if the event factorization theorem already matches the goal.

Each event-factorization statement is almost everywhere for its chosen pair of measurable sets; its exceptional null set can depend on that pair. If a later proof needs one common full-measure set on which a kernel identity holds for every set, use the countability or countable-generation assumptions in the corresponding theorem. Do not exchange an uncountable family of almost-everywhere statements for a single uniform assertion without proving that step.

For integrable real-valued functions, event factorization can lead to conditional expectation identities for products, but extra integrability assumptions are needed. Conditional independence of events does not by itself authorize every multiplication or interchange of integrals. Keep the measure-theoretic assumptions associated with the expectation theorem separate from the independence predicate.

For bounded measurable real functions `u` and `v`, measurable with respect to the two independent σ-algebras, the event factorization extends by approximation with simple functions to

\[
  \mathbb{E}[uv\mid m']
  =\mathbb{E}[u\mid m']\,\mathbb{E}[v\mid m']
  \quad\text{almost surely}.
\]

This is often the form needed in variance and covariance calculations. A formal proof can establish the identity for indicators, extend by linearity to simple functions, and pass to bounded measurable functions using the relevant convergence theorem. For unbounded functions, establish integrability of the product and conditional expectations before applying an extension result.

The distinction between events and expectations helps debug a proof. If the target is a conditional probability, stay with indicators and `condIndepFun_iff_condExp_inter_preimage_eq_mul`. If it is a moment identity, use a theorem formulated for integrable functions or perform the approximation argument explicitly.

In particular, if `u` and `v` are square-integrable and conditionally independent given `m'`, the conditional covariance vanishes:

\[
  \mathbb{E}[uv\mid m']
  -\mathbb{E}[u\mid m']\mathbb{E}[v\mid m']=0
  \quad\text{almost surely}.
\]

This does not say that the unconditional covariance is zero. The hidden-cause example has conditional covariance zero given `Θ`, but positive marginal covariance. The law of total covariance includes the covariance of the conditional means, which can be nonzero when both means depend on the shared information.

## 6. From π-systems to generated σ-algebras

To prove independence of two σ-algebras, it can be enough to check a pair of generating π-systems. Mathlib provides lemmas in both directions between conditional independence of generators and conditional independence of the generated measurable spaces.

This mirrors the unconditioned measure uniqueness pattern. Start with a manageable collection of events—often cylinders, singleton atoms, or coordinate events—prove the conditional factorization there, and extend it to the generated σ-algebras using the π-system theorem. The generating-set proof should state measurability and the π-system property explicitly; the extension theorem cannot infer these structural facts from a collection's name.

For a finite family of variables, `iCondIndepFun` is stronger than checking all pairs independently. Pairwise conditional independence does not generally imply joint conditional independence. The family predicate packages a product formula for every finite subfamily, matching the independence notion needed for joint probability factorizations.

Conditional independence is preserved under measurable transformations of the two variables. If `h` and `k` are measurable, then the information generated by `h ∘ X` is no richer than that generated by `X`, and similarly for `k ∘ Y`. The σ-algebra formulation makes this inheritance transparent: independence of the larger generated σ-algebras implies independence of their measurable substructures. In Lean, prefer a congruence or monotonicity theorem when available; otherwise prove that each transformed preimage event belongs to the original generated σ-algebra.

Two limiting choices of `m'` are useful checks. Conditioning on the bottom σ-algebra reduces conditional independence to ordinary independence under the probability interpretation. Conditioning on the full ambient σ-algebra makes measurable random variables conditionally independent: their indicator events are already known, and the indicator of an intersection is the product of the indicators. Thus the amount of conditioning information can change the truth of the property.

## 7. The conditional-kernel viewpoint

On standard Borel spaces, conditional expectation with respect to `m'` gives a kernel `condExpKernel μ m'`. For a measurable set `A`, the kernel value at `ω` represents a version of the conditional probability of `A` given the information at `ω`. Conditional independence can then be expressed as almost-everywhere factorization of the conditional kernel on intersections.

This connects Chapter 43 to Chapters 41 and 42. A conditional distribution is itself a kernel, and independence becomes an identity between a joint conditional kernel and a product of conditional marginal kernels. The kernel formulation handles many events at once and makes composition with measurable maps available.

Use the conditional-expectation formulation when working with a specific indicator or event. Use conditional kernels when manipulating the full conditional law, composing models, or stating a distributional factorization. Both formulations encode the same concept under the hypotheses of the corresponding equivalence theorem.

The distributional equivalence theorem has additional countability or countable-generation assumptions on the value spaces, while event-factorization lemmas can be applied directly under their own measurability hypotheses. This suggests a practical choice: prove event identities first when the state spaces are complicated, and pass to the conditional-kernel product identity when the codomains have the regularity required by that theorem.

## 8. A proof workflow

1. State the ambient finite measure and the conditioning σ-algebra.
2. If conditioning on a random variable, construct its comap σ-algebra and prove the inclusion relation.
3. Prove measurability of each random variable or event involved.
4. Choose `CondIndepFun`, `CondIndep`, or a set-level predicate to match the target.
5. Use the conditional-expectation factorization theorem for preimage events.
6. For generated σ-algebras, prove the factorization on π-systems and apply the extension result.
7. For a finite family, use the family predicate rather than pairwise independence if joint factorization is required.
8. Keep all factorization equalities almost everywhere and track the conditioning measure.

This ordering keeps the information structure visible. Many failed applications come from having the right probabilistic idea but the wrong comap, an omitted measurability proof, or a conditional independence statement on a different ambient measure.

## 9. Common failure modes

### Conditional independence is confused with ordinary independence

Conditional factorization is checked after applying conditional expectation with respect to the specified information.

### Pairwise independence is used for a family

Pairwise factorization does not give joint factorization for larger finite subfamilies. Use `iCondIndepFun` where needed.

### The conditioning σ-algebra is reversed or omitted

Use the comap of the conditioning variable and prove it lies below the ambient measurable space.

### Pointwise factorization is demanded

Conditional expectations and kernels are versions. The defining equations hold almost everywhere.

### Conditional event factorization is used for arbitrary unbounded functions

Additional integrability or approximation arguments are needed before passing from indicators to products of random variables.

### The ambient space regularity is missing

The current function-level definitions use standard Borel and finite-measure assumptions. Check those against the library theorem before building the proof.

### Compiled conditional-independence statements

The hidden-cause calculation can be checked without rounding by using rational probabilities. Each conditional success probability factors within a hidden-state row, while averaging over the hidden state creates a joint success probability different from the product of the marginals.

```lean
theorem exercise_43_1_hidden_row_zero_factors :
    (1 / 10 : ℚ) * (1 / 10) = 1 / 100 := by
  norm_num

theorem exercise_43_2_hidden_row_one_factors :
    (9 / 10 : ℚ) * (9 / 10) = 81 / 100 := by
  norm_num

theorem exercise_43_3_hidden_marginal_success :
    (1 / 2 : ℚ) * (1 / 10) + (1 / 2) * (9 / 10) = 1 / 2 := by
  norm_num

theorem exercise_43_4_hidden_joint_success :
    (1 / 2 : ℚ) * (1 / 10) * (1 / 10) +
        (1 / 2) * (9 / 10) * (9 / 10) = 41 / 100 := by
  norm_num

theorem exercise_43_5_hidden_variables_not_independent :
    (41 / 100 : ℚ) ≠ (1 / 2) * (1 / 2) := by
  norm_num
```

On the finite Boolean state space, conditional independence given the hidden state can be checked by verifying the factorization on each pair of atoms. The next model builds the conditional row from Bernoulli atom probabilities, derives both marginals by summing out the other coordinate, and proves the cell factorization for every hidden state and pair of outcomes.

```lean
def bernoulliAtom (p : ℚ) (x : Bool) : ℚ := if x then p else 1 - p

def hiddenSuccessProb (θ : Bool) : ℚ := if θ then 9 / 10 else 1 / 10

def hiddenJointAtom (θ x y : Bool) : ℚ :=
  bernoulliAtom (hiddenSuccessProb θ) x * bernoulliAtom (hiddenSuccessProb θ) y

def hiddenXMarginal (θ x : Bool) : ℚ := ∑ y : Bool, hiddenJointAtom θ x y

def hiddenYMarginal (θ y : Bool) : ℚ := ∑ x : Bool, hiddenJointAtom θ x y

theorem bernoulliAtom_total (p : ℚ) : ∑ x : Bool, bernoulliAtom p x = 1 := by
  simp [bernoulliAtom]

theorem hiddenXMarginal_eq (θ x : Bool) :
    hiddenXMarginal θ x = bernoulliAtom (hiddenSuccessProb θ) x := by
  unfold hiddenXMarginal hiddenJointAtom
  rw [← Finset.mul_sum, bernoulliAtom_total]
  ring

theorem hiddenYMarginal_eq (θ y : Bool) :
    hiddenYMarginal θ y = bernoulliAtom (hiddenSuccessProb θ) y := by
  unfold hiddenYMarginal hiddenJointAtom
  rw [← Finset.sum_mul, bernoulliAtom_total]
  ring

theorem hiddenJoint_conditional_independence (θ x y : Bool) :
    hiddenJointAtom θ x y = hiddenXMarginal θ x * hiddenYMarginal θ y := by
  rw [hiddenXMarginal_eq, hiddenYMarginal_eq, hiddenJointAtom]
```

## Summary

Conditional independence factors conditional probabilities after information has been fixed. Mathlib provides predicates for events, measurable spaces, functions, and finite families, together with characterizations through conditional expectation and conditional kernels. The conditioning structure, measurability, finite measure, and standard Borel assumptions should be explicit. Conditional independence can hold even when the variables are marginally dependent, and family independence requires more than pairwise checks.
