# Chapter 34 — Probability Measures and Random Variables

> **Audience.** This chapter assumes familiarity with measurable spaces, measures, integrals, and product measures. It develops probability-measure structures, random variables as measurable maps, laws, independence, and expectations.

Probability theory in Lean is built on the measure-theory interfaces from Chapters 31–33. A probability space is a measure space whose total mass is one. A random variable is a measurable function out of that space, and its distribution is the pushforward measure. Expectations are integrals. Independence is a statement about the measurable structures generated by random variables, with event-level and integral-level characterizations available when they suit the proof.

This chapter focuses on representing common probability arguments through those interfaces. It moves from events and distributions to independence and moments, then connects convergence of distributions to weak convergence of probability measures.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to:

- represent a probability space using `IsProbabilityMeasure` or `ProbabilityMeasure Ω`;
- state random variables and their laws using measurability and pushforward measures;
- express expectation as a Bochner integral and reduce discrete expectations to sums;
- use `IndepFun` and `iIndepFun` rather than unfolding independence definitions;
- connect weak convergence with convergence of bounded continuous expectations.

## 1. Probability measures

A measure `P : Measure Ω` is a probability measure when `P Set.univ = 1`. Mathlib records this property as the typeclass `[IsProbabilityMeasure P]`. This is the right assumption when a theorem works with a measure and needs probability-specific facts such as finite total mass or normalization.

For constructions that treat probability measures as mathematical objects, Mathlib also defines `ProbabilityMeasure Ω`. This is a subtype of measures carrying the probability property. The distinction is useful: a general measure can acquire an `IsProbabilityMeasure` instance in a local context, while a `ProbabilityMeasure` value carries the fact as part of its type and can vary as an argument to a continuous map.

A probability measure is finite because its mass on the whole space is one. Consequently, bounded measurable real-valued functions are integrable, and finite-measure integral results can often be applied directly. Unbounded variables still require an integrability argument; finite total mass controls the measure of the domain, not the size of the function.

```text
sample space Ω + measurable sets + P(Ω) = 1
                       │
                       ▼
               random variable X
                       │ pushforward
                       ▼
               law of X on value space
```

The probability of an event is just its measure under `P`, with the measurability condition needed when applying countable additivity or probability identities. `P Set.univ = 1` gives the normalization; it does not make every subset measurable.

Indicators connect events to expectations. The indicator of a measurable event is a random variable taking the value one on the event and zero outside, and its expectation is the probability of that event. This lets event-probability calculations use integral linearity and almost-everywhere equality, while event independence can be translated into factorization of indicator expectations.

## 2. Random variables and their laws

A random variable with values in a measurable space `E` is a measurable function `X : Ω → E`. In probability applications, `Ω` is the sample space and `E` is the value space. The law or distribution of `X` is the pushforward `Measure.map X P`, which measures events in `E` by pulling them back along `X`.

This definition keeps the probability model and the observed value space separate. Two random variables can have different sample spaces and still have the same law. A theorem about distribution should therefore compare pushforward measures or use an `IdentDistrib`/`HasLaw` interface, rather than compare the underlying functions pointwise.

Equality in distribution is weaker than equality of random variables. It preserves every statistic that is determined by the law and is integrable, but it does not identify outcomes on the underlying sample space. This matters when a theorem asks for almost-sure equality, a coupling, or a pathwise property: distributional equality alone does not supply those stronger conclusions.

Measurability is essential because events in the value space must pull back to measurable events in the sample space. If only an almost-everywhere measurable representative is available, use the API that works with `AEMeasurable`; do not force an everywhere-measurable version without a reason.

The law construction is also compatible with composition. If `g : E → F` is measurable, the law of `g ∘ X` is the pushforward of the law of `X` along `g`. This is the measure-theoretic form of applying a measurable function to a random variable. It is frequently called the mapping or transformation principle for distributions.

## 3. Expectation is integration

For an integrable real-valued random variable `X`, its expectation is the integral

\[
  \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X(\omega)\,dP(\omega).
\]

For vector-valued random variables, the Bochner integral gives the same construction in a complete normed vector space. Integrability matters: finite total probability guarantees integrability of bounded measurable functions, but an unbounded random variable may have infinite first moment or may fail to be integrable.

Expectation depends only on the law. Integrating a function of `X` under `P` can be transported to an integral over the law of `X`, when the relevant integrability and measurability conditions hold. This lets a proof move from a complicated sample space to a simpler distribution on the value space.

The `PMF` interface handles discrete distributions. On a countable type with measurable singletons, expectation with respect to a probability mass function is a weighted sum:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_34_3_pmf_expectation
    {α E : Type*} [MeasurableSpace α] [MeasurableSingletonClass α]
    [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace ℝ E] [CompleteSpace E]
    (p : PMF α) (f : α → E) (hf : Integrable f p.toMeasure) :
    ∫ a, f a ∂p.toMeasure = ∑' a, (p a).toReal • f a := by
  exact PMF.integral_eq_tsum p f hf
```

When the sample space is finite, the corresponding theorem gives a finite sum. These formulas are often the bridge between abstract expectation statements and concrete combinatorics or numerical calculation.

## 4. Independence of events and random variables

Two events `A` and `B` are independent when the probability of their intersection is the product of their probabilities. For random variables, independence means that every measurable event determined by one variable is independent of every measurable event determined by the other.

Mathlib packages this as `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun X Y P`. Its event characterization has the form

\[
  P(X^{-1}(S) \cap Y^{-1}(T))
  = P(X^{-1}(S))\,P(Y^{-1}(T))
\]

for measurable sets `S` and `T` in the codomains. This definition uses the measurable structures generated by the two functions, which is why the theorem does not depend on a chosen coordinate representation of either random variable.

For a family of random variables, use `ProbabilityTheory.iIndepFun`. It records joint independence of every finite subfamily. Finite-subfamily statements are the right level for infinite families: probability measures are countably additive, and independence of an infinite collection is defined through all finite collections rather than an infinite product equation in one step.

Independence is preserved by measurable functions of independent random variables. If `X` and `Y` are independent and `f` and `g` are measurable, then `f ∘ X` and `g ∘ Y` remain independent. The theorem follows because each transformed variable generates no more event information than its source. This closure property is often more useful than unfolding the event characterization.

For proofs about independence, first use `IndepFun` as a stable proposition. If the goal concerns two concrete events, switch to the theorem about measurable preimages. If the goal concerns functions of independent variables, use the composition closure theorem. This staged approach prevents a global independence hypothesis from being expanded into quantified event sets before the proof needs them.

## 5. Product spaces give a model of independence

If `(Ω, P)` and `(Ω', Q)` are probability spaces, their product measure is again a probability measure. The coordinate random variables on `Ω × Ω'` are independent. Mathlib provides `ProbabilityTheory.indepFun_prod` for this construction.

This result gives a canonical model for independent variables: place each variable on its own probability space, then consider both as coordinate functions on the product space. In many applications the variables already live on a shared sample space; `IndepFun` lets one state independence there without changing the underlying model.

The product-space construction also explains why products of independent laws appear. The joint law of the coordinate pair is the product of the marginal laws under the appropriate measurability assumptions. Conversely, if a pair has a product law, the coordinate variables are independent. These statements connect pushforwards, product measures, and event-level independence.

## 6. Independence and expectation factorization

For integrable real-valued random variables, independence often lets expectations of products factor:

\[
  \mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X] \, \mathbb{E}[Y],
\]

under suitable integrability assumptions. More generally, expectations of separate functions of independent variables factor when the relevant products are integrable. The assumptions prevent undefined products such as `0 * ∞` at the level of extended expectations.

Moment-generating functions package exponential expectations. For independent variables, the moment-generating function of a sum factors into the product of the individual moment-generating functions when these expectations are defined. Mathlib has results such as `ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun.mgf_add` and finite-family variants. These can support Chernoff-style probability bounds, where an exponential moment controls a tail event.

The proof-design pattern is to establish independence once, prove the required integrability of the transformed variables, then apply the factorization theorem. Do not rederive independence for every measurable transformation or expand the exponential product by hand if the library already exposes the appropriate theorem.

This factorization supports tail estimates. Markov's inequality applied to an exponential of a random variable bounds the probability of a large deviation in terms of its moment-generating function. For independent sums, factorization reduces the moment-generating function to a product, which often yields an exponential upper bound. Mathlib provides Chernoff-style bounds with explicit assumptions on finite measure, sign of the parameter, and integrability of the exponential. Keep those hypotheses visible: finiteness of ordinary moments does not automatically imply existence of every exponential moment.

## 7. Moments and discrete probability

The `ProbabilityTheory.moment X p μ` interface represents the `p`th moment of a real random variable. Central moments measure deviation from the expectation, and moment-generating or cumulant-generating functions encode families of moments and exponential tails. The API includes assumptions about integrability because these quantities need not exist for every random variable.

For finite probability spaces, a moment calculation reduces to a finite weighted sum. For countable PMFs, it becomes a `tsum`. This connects probability to the finite-sum and infinite-series interfaces from earlier chapters. The key steps are to establish integrability, rewrite the expectation as a weighted sum, and then use algebraic simplification or summability theorems.

Avoid treating a probability mass function as an arbitrary function into `[0,1]`. `PMF α` packages nonnegative weights whose total mass is one. Its associated measure and integrals already know how those weights contribute to expectations.

## 8. Convergence in distribution

Convergence in distribution, also called weak convergence of probability measures, can be expressed by convergence of expectations against every bounded continuous test function. Mathlib's `ProbabilityMeasure Ω` carries a topology of weak convergence when the measurable structure is suitably compatible with the topology.

This interface separates two levels of convergence. Almost-everywhere or pointwise convergence compares random variables on a common sample space. Convergence in distribution compares their laws, potentially when the variables live on different spaces. Convergence of bounded continuous expectations is the stable formulation for the latter.

Use the probability-measure topology when a theorem is about varying distributions as points in a topological space. Use pushforward or `HasLaw` statements when the theorem compares particular random variables. The distinction prevents a proof from requiring a coupling or shared sample space that the mathematics does not provide.

The pushforward operation itself behaves continuously on probability measures when the map between value spaces is continuous and the measurable structures have the required Borel compatibility. This gives a reusable route to convergence of transformed distributions: prove convergence of the original laws, then apply the continuous map. If the transformation is only measurable, the pushforward is still defined, but continuity of the induced map on probability measures is not automatic.

## 9. A proof workflow

1. State the sample-space measure and install `[IsProbabilityMeasure P]` when probability normalization is needed.
2. Give each random variable an explicit measurable codomain.
3. Use `Measure.map` or a law interface to move from a random variable to its distribution.
4. Express expectation as a Bochner integral and prove integrability of unbounded quantities.
5. Use `IndepFun` or `iIndepFun` for independence; switch to event or integral characterizations only when useful.
6. For discrete models, use `PMF` and convert expectations with its sum or `tsum` theorem.
7. For distributional limits, compare expectations of bounded continuous test functions or use the `ProbabilityMeasure` topology.

This workflow keeps the probability model, value distribution, and analytic operation distinct. It also makes hidden assumptions—measurability, integrability, finiteness, and independence—visible at the point where each theorem needs them.

## 10. Common failure modes

### A random variable is treated as measurable by definition

The function must be measurable into its codomain's measurable space. State or prove this before using its law.

### A distribution is identified with the sample space

The law is a pushforward measure on the value space. It can differ from the original probability measure's domain.

### Expectation is used for a nonintegrable variable

Prove integrability or use an extended nonnegative integral for nonnegative quantities.

### Independence is unfolded into all event pairs

Use `IndepFun`; apply its event characterization only when specific events are involved.

### Independence of a family is stated only pairwise

Pairwise independence does not imply joint independence. Use `iIndepFun` when the argument requires finite joint subfamilies.

### A limit theorem assumes a common sample space

For convergence in distribution, compare laws or bounded continuous expectations rather than forcing pointwise convergence on one space.

### 11.1 Compiled probability patterns

The first exercise is the defining total-mass property of a probability measure. The typeclass lets `simp` discharge the identity directly:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_34_1_probability_univ
    {Ω : Type*} [MeasurableSpace Ω] (P : Measure Ω) [IsProbabilityMeasure P] :
    P Set.univ = 1 := by
  simp
```

The law of a measurable random variable is the pushforward measure. Evaluating it on a measurable event pulls that event back to the sample space:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_34_2_law_preimage
    {Ω E : Type*} [MeasurableSpace Ω] [MeasurableSpace E]
    (P : Measure Ω) (X : Ω → E) (hX : Measurable X)
    (s : Set E) (hs : MeasurableSet s) :
    Measure.map X P s = P (X ⁻¹' s) := by
  exact Measure.map_apply hX hs
```

Exercise 4 is the measurable-event characterization of independence. It exposes the exact preimage events whose measures must factor:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_34_4_indepFun_events
    {Ω E F : Type*} [MeasurableSpace Ω] [MeasurableSpace E] [MeasurableSpace F]
    (P : Measure Ω) (X : Ω → E) (Y : Ω → F) :
    ProbabilityTheory.IndepFun X Y P ↔
      ∀ s t, MeasurableSet s → MeasurableSet t →
        P (X ⁻¹' s ∩ Y ⁻¹' t) = P (X ⁻¹' s) * P (Y ⁻¹' t) := by
  exact ProbabilityTheory.indepFun_iff_measure_inter_preimage_eq_mul
```

## Summary

A probability measure is a measure of total mass one, recorded either by `IsProbabilityMeasure` or by the bundled type `ProbabilityMeasure`. Random variables are measurable maps, their laws are pushforward measures, and expectations are Bochner integrals. `IndepFun` and `iIndepFun` package event-level and finite-family independence. Product probability spaces provide a canonical model of independent coordinates. For discrete models, PMF expectations become weighted sums; for distributional limits, weak convergence is characterized by bounded continuous test functions.
