# Chapter 33 — Product Measures and Fubini's Theorem

> **Audience.** This chapter assumes familiarity with measurable spaces, integrable functions, and Bochner integration. It develops product measures, Tonelli's theorem for nonnegative functions, and Fubini's theorem for integrable functions.

Many analysis arguments begin with a function of two variables and end with an iterated integral. The formal proof must keep track of three objects: the product measurable space, the product measure, and the integrability or nonnegativity condition that allows the order of integration to change. Mathlib supplies separate interfaces for each. The product measure is not merely a notational pairing of two measures; it is built so that sections and iterated integration interact with countable additivity.

This chapter continues the filter and integration techniques from Chapters 31 and 32. It emphasizes the different roles of Tonelli and Fubini, the σ-finiteness assumptions used by their APIs, and the proof-state advantages of choosing curried or uncurried functions deliberately.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to:

- use the product measure `μ.prod ν` and understand its rectangle rule;
- choose Tonelli for nonnegative extended-valued functions and Fubini for integrable vector-valued functions;
- state the integrability criterion on a product space;
- move between `α × β → E` and `α → β → E` forms;
- track the s-finiteness assumptions required by current product-measure theorems.

## 1. Product measurable spaces and measures

Given measurable spaces on `α` and `β`, their product measurable space on `α × β` is generated by measurable rectangles. The coordinate projections are measurable, and measurable functions of either coordinate can be lifted to the product. This lets a function of two variables be analyzed using the same composition principles as other measurable functions.

The product measure `μ.prod ν` is a measure on `α × β`. On a measurable rectangle `s ×ˢ t`, it satisfies

\[
  (\mu \mathbin{.prod} \nu)(s \times t) = \mu(s)\,\nu(t).
\]

This identity is the basic calibration point: a product measure combines the two marginal measures independently on rectangles. The API extends the construction to general measurable sets by integrating the measure of each section.

```text
section over β at x  ── measure under ν ──▶  x ↦ ν({y | (x,y) ∈ s})
                                                     │
                                                     ▼ integrate under μ
                                      (μ.prod ν)(s)
```

The exact product-measure theorems commonly require an s-finite measure. Many standard measures are σ-finite, which supplies this property. Other results work with weaker assumptions, but do not infer all rectangle and integration identities from the existence of `μ.prod ν` alone. Read the theorem's measure hypotheses, especially when working with an abstract measure that is not known to be finite or σ-finite.

There is a subtle implementation boundary here: the product measure is defined for arbitrary measures, but its defining formula uses a zero measure when the second factor is not s-finite. Thus a theorem that constructs `μ.prod ν` can elaborate without a finiteness instance even though the resulting measure may be the library's fallback value. Theorems describing the intended product behavior require the appropriate `SFinite` hypotheses. Treat those instances as part of the mathematical context, not as optional performance hints.

## 2. Sections and the measure of a product set

For a set `s : Set (α × β)` and a point `x : α`, its section is the set of `y` for which `(x, y)` lies in `s`. Product-measure construction reduces the measure of `s` to the extended nonnegative integral of the measures of these sections:

\[
  (\mu \mathbin{.prod} \nu)(s)
  = \int^- x,\; \nu(\{y \mid (x,y) \in s\})\, d\mu.
\]

For a measurable `s`, measurability of the section-measure function is what makes this `lintegral` meaningful. The theorem `Measure.prod_apply` packages this. A symmetric theorem reverses the roles of `α` and `β`.

For rectangles, every section is either all of `t` or empty, so the section formula simplifies to the product of the two measures. The theorem `Measure.prod_prod` records this result. It is often a good first lemma in a proof about a product measure, because it translates the abstract measure into a familiar scalar expression.

Sections also let product-measure statements express almost-everywhere information. Under the measurable-set and s-finiteness hypotheses, a property holds almost everywhere for the product measure exactly when it holds for almost every `x` and then for almost every `y` in the section. This is the measure-theoretic justification for nested “for almost every” reasoning. The measurability condition cannot be omitted in the converse: an arbitrary nonmeasurable subset of a null set need not itself be a measurable product event.

## 3. Tonelli's theorem for nonnegative functions

Tonelli's theorem applies to a measurable function into `ℝ≥0∞`. It states that integrating over the product is the same as iterating the two nonnegative integrals, even when the value is infinite:

\[
  \int^- z, f(z)\,d(\mu \mathbin{.prod} \nu)
  = \int^- x,\;\int^- y, f(x,y)\,d\nu\,d\mu.
\]

The nonnegative codomain is essential. It provides an ordered setting where increasing limits and infinite values are meaningful. Unlike Bochner Fubini, Tonelli does not require that the total integral already be finite. Instead, it identifies the possibly infinite values on both sides.

Use the Tonelli interface when the integrand is nonnegative or has been converted into an `ℝ≥0∞`-valued function. For a real-valued signed function, applying Tonelli directly is a type error and, mathematically, the theorem does not apply. One may sometimes split into positive and negative parts, or use an integrability argument and Fubini instead.

## 4. Fubini's theorem for Bochner integrals

Fubini's theorem applies to a function `f : α × β → E` that is integrable with respect to `μ.prod ν`. Under the product-measure assumptions, it identifies the product integral with an iterated Bochner integral:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_33_3_fubini
    {α β E : Type*} [MeasurableSpace α] [MeasurableSpace β]
    {μ : Measure α} {ν : Measure β}
    [SFinite μ] [SFinite ν]
    [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace ℝ E]
    (f : α × β → E) (hf : Integrable f (μ.prod ν)) :
    ∫ z, f z ∂(μ.prod ν) = ∫ x, ∫ y, f (x, y) ∂ν ∂μ := by
  exact integral_prod f hf
```

The theorem does not simply exchange integral symbols. It also establishes that the inner integral is measurable and that the outer integral is well-defined. The input `hf` is the central analytic hypothesis. If it is unavailable, the next question is whether it can be proved using the product integrability criterion or whether a nonnegative Tonelli theorem better matches the goal.

There is a symmetric theorem with the order of integration reversed. If both measures satisfy the relevant hypotheses, equality of the two iterated integrals follows by comparing each with the same product integral. This is clearer than attempting a direct transformation between the iterated expressions.

Products that separate by coordinate have an even shorter interface. For scalar functions `f : α → L` and `g : β → L`, the theorem `integral_prod_mul` identifies the product-space integral of `f x * g y` with the product of their separate integrals. The analogous bilinear theorem handles vector-valued functions and continuous bilinear maps. These results are useful for factorized densities and product distributions; they encode the integrability and continuity bookkeeping that would otherwise surround a Fubini proof.

When the measures are finite and the integrand is bounded, integrability often follows from a simple norm estimate: the norm is bounded by a constant, and the product space has finite total mass. For unbounded functions, this shortcut is unavailable. Typical replacements are a direct integrable-norm bound, a section criterion, or a theorem showing that a product of integrable one-variable functions remains integrable.

## 5. Integrability on a product

For a strongly measurable or almost-everywhere strongly measurable function, product integrability can be characterized using sections. One version says that `f` is integrable on `μ.prod ν` exactly when:

1. for almost every `x`, the section `y ↦ f (x, y)` is integrable under `ν`;
2. the function `x ↦ ∫ y, ‖f (x, y)‖ ∂ν` is integrable under `μ`.

The norm appears in the second condition because it controls absolute integrability. Requiring only that the signed inner integrals exist would permit cancellation that hides a nonintegrable norm. The integral of the norm is the quantity that supports Fubini's theorem for vector-valued functions.

The hypotheses and orientation of this criterion matter. The right-hand version asks for almost-everywhere integrability of sections in `β` and an outer integrability bound in `α`; a symmetric version swaps the variables and measures. Choose the version that matches the sections already available in the proof.

The equivalence also includes an almost-everywhere strong-measurability hypothesis on the product function. Section integrability and a finite norm integral alone do not establish that an arbitrary function is measurable. In formal proofs, prove measurability first, then apply the criterion to the remaining analytic conditions. This order often allows the first hypothesis to be discharged by a separate composition or continuity argument.

## 6. Curried and uncurried function forms

Mathematical notation commonly writes a two-variable function as `f x y`, with type `α → β → E`. Product-space theorems often use `f : α × β → E`. The conversion is `Function.uncurry`; in the other direction, a function on pairs can be written as `fun x y => f (x, y)`.

Mathlib provides variants of several theorems in both forms. A curried theorem may ask for measurability of `Function.uncurry f`; an uncurried theorem may ask you to provide the pair-valued function explicitly. Pick one representation at the start of a proof and avoid repeatedly changing forms. If an API theorem has the wrong shape, use `Function.uncurry` or `Function.curry` at the boundary rather than reworking the whole argument.

This is also a typeclass-inference issue. Lean can often infer the pair function from an explicit `f : α × β → E`, but a curried notation may leave the intended section ambiguous. Named arguments and local definitions can make the chosen orientation clear.

## 7. Measurability of iterated integrals

An iterated integral is a function of the outer variable. To integrate it again, Lean must know that the inner integral varies measurably. Mathlib has theorems showing that integrating one variable of a strongly measurable function produces a strongly measurable function of the remaining variable, under a suitable s-finiteness condition.

This measurability theorem is one of the hidden steps in Fubini. In elementary calculations it can be tempting to treat `x ↦ ∫ y, f (x,y) ∂ν` as obviously measurable. For arbitrary measurable spaces and vector targets, it needs proof. When the final goal is only the product-integral equality, `integral_prod` packages the measurability step. When the inner integral is itself an object of interest, use the section-measurability theorem directly.

An especially useful simplification occurs when the integrand depends on only one coordinate. The product integral of `f x` is the integral of `f` multiplied by the total mass of the other factor. Mathlib provides `integral_fun_fst` and `integral_fun_snd` for these cases. Besides shortening calculations, these lemmas reveal whether the factor measure is finite: an infinite total mass may make a constant extension nonintegrable unless the integral vanishes in the appropriate sense.

The same Fubini pattern applies to a rectangle `s ×ˢ t` using set integrals. The theorem `setIntegral_prod` requires integrability on that rectangle, then expresses the product set integral as two iterated set integrals. Keeping the domain restriction in the set-integral notation is often clearer than multiplying by two indicator functions and proving their measurability separately.

For three or more variables, build the product iteratively and reassociate it using the product associativity equivalence. A finite-dimensional integral is then a sequence of binary Fubini steps. Each step changes the grouping of variables but preserves the same underlying product measure up to the canonical equivalence. Name the grouping explicitly when Lean's nested pair types become difficult to read.

## 8. A proof strategy for double integrals

1. Put the function in the form `α × β → E` or `α → β → E` that matches the desired theorem.
2. Decide whether the integrand is nonnegative (`lintegral`) or integrable and possibly signed (`integral`).
3. Establish the product-measure assumptions, usually s-finiteness or σ-finiteness of both factors.
4. For Fubini, prove `Integrable f (μ.prod ν)`; use the section criterion when it is not already known.
5. Apply `integral_prod` or its symmetric form instead of proving the iterated equality directly.
6. If a product-set measure is involved, simplify with `Measure.prod_prod`.

If the desired statement changes the order of integration, compare each order to the product integral. This gives a short proof with a visible reason both expressions exist.

For a separated product `f x * g y`, establish integrability of the one-variable factors first; the API has lemmas that build product integrability and compute the integral from those factors. For vector-valued constructions, use the continuous bilinear-map version rather than trying to coerce the product into scalar multiplication. This is a recurring proof-design pattern: identify the structure-preserving map on the codomain, then let its continuity theorem transport integrability and integration.

For an arbitrary `f x y`, avoid guessing that section integrability is enough. The integrated norm must itself be integrable in the outer variable, and the product function must satisfy the measurability premise. These are exactly the conditions exposed by `integrable_prod_iff`; working from that statement is more reliable than trying to invoke Fubini and hoping typeclass search discovers the missing facts.

## 9. Common failure modes

### Fubini is applied without product integrability

Prove integrability first. For signed or vector-valued functions, pointwise convergence or measurability alone is not enough.

### Tonelli is used for a signed integrand

Tonelli's direct interface is for nonnegative extended-real values. Use Fubini for integrable functions or split the function appropriately.

### The product measure has insufficient finiteness assumptions

Check the theorem for `SFinite` or `SigmaFinite` hypotheses. Some identities do not hold in their usual form for completely arbitrary measures.

### The theorem expects a function on pairs, but the proof has sections

Use `Function.uncurry` or select the curried theorem. Keep the conversion local.

### The iterated integral's measurability is taken for granted

Use the section-integral measurability theorem or apply a Fubini result that supplies it as part of the conclusion.

### 10.1 Compiled solutions for product identities

The first exercise asks for the measure of a rectangle. Under the s-finiteness hypothesis used by the product-measure API, Mathlib proves the identity even for sets that are not known to be measurable:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_33_1_product_rectangle
    {α β : Type*} [MeasurableSpace α] [MeasurableSpace β]
    {μ : Measure α} {ν : Measure β} [SFinite ν]
    (s : Set α) (t : Set β) :
    μ.prod ν (s ×ˢ t) = μ s * ν t := by
  simp
```

Tonelli's theorem uses `ℝ≥0∞`, so the iterated integral is allowed to be infinite. The measurability premise is almost-everywhere measurability with respect to the product measure:

```lean
open MeasureTheory
open scoped ENNReal

theorem exercise_33_2_tonelli
    {α β : Type*} [MeasurableSpace α] [MeasurableSpace β]
    {μ : Measure α} {ν : Measure β} [SFinite ν]
    (f : α × β → ℝ≥0∞) (hf : AEMeasurable f (μ.prod ν)) :
    ∫⁻ z, f z ∂(μ.prod ν) = ∫⁻ x, ∫⁻ y, f (x, y) ∂ν ∂μ := by
  exact lintegral_prod f hf
```

The Fubini theorem above formalizes the third exercise. Its symmetric form proves the change of order in Exercise 6 by identifying both iterated integrals with the product integral:

```lean
open MeasureTheory

theorem exercise_33_6_change_order
    {α β E : Type*} [MeasurableSpace α] [MeasurableSpace β]
    {μ : Measure α} {ν : Measure β}
    [SFinite μ] [SFinite ν]
    [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace ℝ E]
    (f : α × β → E) (hf : Integrable f (μ.prod ν)) :
    ∫ x, ∫ y, f (x, y) ∂ν ∂μ = ∫ y, ∫ x, f (x, y) ∂μ ∂ν := by
  exact integral_integral_swap hf
```

## Summary

The product measure combines marginal measures and is characterized through measurable sections; on rectangles it multiplies the side measures. Tonelli iterates nonnegative extended-valued integrals, including infinite values. Fubini iterates Bochner integrals for product-integrable functions and also handles measurability of the inner integral. Product integrability can be checked from almost-everywhere integrable sections and an integrable outer norm bound. Track s-finiteness, integrability, and curried versus uncurried function types explicitly.
